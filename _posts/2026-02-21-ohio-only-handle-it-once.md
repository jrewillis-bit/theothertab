<p>Most clutter isn’t caused by too much stuff. It’s caused by <strong>half-decisions</strong>.</p>

<p>Paper moved from one surface to another. Emails read but left “to deal with”. Items put down “for now” and never finished.</p>

<p>The simplest idea I’ve used to stop that is:</p>

<p><strong>OHIO — Only Handle It Once.</strong></p>

<p>It’s not about being perfect. It’s about reducing friction and stopping clutter at the source.</p>

<h2>What is OHIO (Only Handle It Once)?</h2>

<p>OHIO stands for <strong>Only Handle It Once</strong>. When something lands in your life — a message, a letter, a task, an item — you aim to deal with it fully the first time you touch it.</p>

<p>In practice, that means making an immediate decision:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Do it</strong> (complete the action)</li>
  <li><strong>File it</strong> (store it properly)</li>
  <li><strong>Schedule it</strong> (capture a specific next step)</li>
  <li><strong>Bin it</strong> (delete/recycle/donate)</li>
</ul>

<p>What OHIO tries to eliminate is the dangerous middle option: <em>“I’ll deal with this later.”</em></p>

<h2>Why handling things twice creates clutter</h2>

<p>Every extra “touch” costs attention.</p>

<p>When you move something without deciding what it is or what happens next, you haven’t reduced your workload — you’ve just delayed it and added mental noise.</p>

<p>That’s how you end up with:</p>

<ul>
  <li>paper piles</li>
  <li>overflowing inboxes</li>
  <li>bits of admin hanging around your head</li>
  <li>the low-level stress of unfinished small things</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Clutter is often unresolved decisions in physical form.</strong></p>

<h2>How to use OHIO day-to-day</h2>

<h3>1) Post and paperwork</h3>

<p>When post comes through the door, I avoid creating a “later pile”.</p>

<p>I handle it once:</p>

<ul>
  <li>if it needs action, I do it immediately (or write a clear next step)</li>
  <li>if I need to keep it, it goes into its proper home</li>
  <li>if I don’t, it’s recycled there and then</li>
</ul>

<p>Most paperwork needs about 30 seconds of attention. The problem is when it gets 30 seconds <em>four times</em>.</p>

<h3>2) Email (where OHIO pays off fastest)</h3>

<p>Email is a clutter factory because it’s easy to re-open the same message repeatedly.</p>

<p>When I open an email, I decide straight away:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Reply now</strong> (if it’s quick)</li>
  <li><strong>Archive</strong> (if it’s done)</li>
  <li><strong>Add a task</strong> with a specific next step (and then archive it)</li>
  <li><strong>Delete</strong> (if it’s not useful)</li>
</ul>

<p>Unread emails aren’t the issue. <strong>Undecided emails</strong> are.</p>

<h3>3) New stuff coming into the house</h3>

<p>If something comes in, it gets a home immediately.</p>

<p>Not “on the side”. Not “for now”.</p>

<p>If I can’t decide where it lives, that’s a signal: either I don’t need it, or I don’t have a system for it yet.</p>

<h3>4) Small tasks</h3>

<p>If something takes a minute or two, I do it there and then.</p>

<p>Not because I’m disciplined — because doing it later means I’ll have to think about it twice.</p>

<p>OHIO isn’t about doing more. It’s about <strong>finishing</strong> more.</p>

<h2>Where OHIO doesn’t apply</h2>

<p>OHIO is not for big, emotional, or complex decisions.</p>

<p>It works best for the small repeatable things that quietly drain attention: messages, paper, admin, and everyday clutter triggers.</p>

<p>Use it selectively and it feels light. Try to apply it to everything and it becomes a stick to beat yourself with.</p>

<h2>Why OHIO reduces stress more than tidying</h2>

<p>Tidying treats the symptom. OHIO treats the cause.</p>

<p>Most clutter isn’t “mess”. It’s <strong>postponed decisions</strong>.</p>

<p>Handle it once, and you’ll notice:</p>

<ul>
  <li>surfaces stay clearer</li>
  <li>your inbox shrinks</li>
  <li>your head feels quieter</li>
</ul>

<h2>Final thought</h2>

<p>OHIO isn’t about productivity. It’s about respecting your time and attention.</p>

<p><strong>Handle it once. Decide. Move on.</strong></p>

<p>Life gets lighter when fewer things are left hanging.</p>
